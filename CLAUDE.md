# DESUU: gum-launcher desk robot (joystick first, face tracking later)

Context for Claude Code sessions: this file is the map and the rules; the details live in `docs/`. When a decision or a number changes, update the doc **and** this summary.

## What we're building

A friendly, 3D-printed desktop robot with a pan-tilt turret head that tosses one hard-coated gum pellet into a person's mouth at 0.5–2 m. Hardware and software are built from scratch, at home, in India. The work runs in two phases (D-011, the owner's decision on 2026-11-25):

- **Phase 1, joystick (now).** An operator aims with a wired joystick over a sight on the head, sets the range, holds ENABLE and presses FIRE. The microcontroller adds the ballistic holdover and runs all the fire control. There is **no camera, no Raspberry Pi and no face tracking** in phase 1.
- **Phase 2, face tracking (later).** A Pi 5 and a camera on the head track the face; after an opt-in (eyebrows up) and a mouth held open for 300 ms, the robot aims and fires on its own, with the eye-zone gate. The joystick stays as the trick-shot controller.

## Safety invariants

Never violate these in code, firmware or hardware. Where a rule differs by phase, both forms are given; neither may be weakened.

1. **Hardware chain.** The ARM switch and E-stop sit in series with actuator power. Nothing may bypass them, and firmware never emulates "armed".
2. **One firing path.** Firing goes only through the fire-control state machine, with every gate passing: REQ-SAF-017 in phase 1 (in the microcontroller), REQ-SAF-001 in the same frame in phase 2. No debug or back-door FIRE path, including in manual trick-shot mode, and nothing received over USB can fire in phase 1.
3. **Eyes.** Phase 1: nothing can see the eyes, so **everyone in front of the armed robot wears impact-rated glasses, every session, no exception** (REQ-SAF-018). Phase 2: safety is judged on the predicted impact zone (3σ plus the worst allowed over-speed) against live eye landmarks + 15 mm. In both, never shrink the margins, the 5 mm aim-below offset or the short range bias to "improve hit rate".
4. **Energy.** ≤ 40 mJ physically for the heaviest configured pellet; ≤ 30 mJ operating. The chronograph locks out above +8 % or on 2 % drift. No code may raise the spring preload beyond the verified cap.
5. **Consent and pacing.** Phase 1: the target says they're ready; ENABLE (deadman) held ≥ 1 s plus a fresh FIRE press; releasing ENABLE aborts; ≥ 10 s between any two shots and at most 5 per arming. Phase 2: an open mouth alone never fires: an explicit opt-in opens a 5 s window for one shot; shots to one person are ≥ 10 s apart, at most 5 per session, and the mouth must close between shots.
6. **Safe defaults.** Boot, reset, controller disconnect and every fault go to SAFE; re-arming needs the switch cycled; nothing auto-resumes. The spring decocks when idle, and the breech empties without power.
7. **Microcontroller checks of its own.** Always: the armed rail, chambered and parked, the minimum interval, and a watchdog. Phase 1: the deadman, fresh-press FIRE and controller-disconnect detection. Phase 2: a 200 ms heartbeat timeout and single-use fresh FIRE tokens from the Pi.
8. **No lasers.** No aiming lasers, ever (the phase 1 sight is printed). Range sensors only as unmodified Class 1 modules.
9. **Tests.** Any change to the gates or interlocks ships with tests (100 % branch coverage of fire control) and a note in docs/safety.md.
10. **People.** Phase 1: impact-rated glasses always (invariant 3). Phase 2: glasses for anyone in front of an armed robot until M10's eye-gate tests pass. Armed testing always starts on printed face targets.

## Architecture in one screen

**Phase 1 (joystick):**

- **Controller:** a wired handheld box on a 2 m Cat-6 cable: KY-023 thumb joystick (rate control), ENABLE (deadman), FIRE, RANGE −/+. Open circuit reads as released (D-011, REQ-CTL-001).
- **Microcontroller** (the owner's Pico or ESP32) does everything:
  - fire control (REQ-SAF-017), as pure Python tested on the PC;
  - joystick rate control, holdover tilt from the ballistic table;
  - turret steppers (step pulses, ramps, homing), launcher motor (JGY370 worm through a TB6612FNG), shuttle servo (MG90S), chamber IR, chronograph gates (edge timestamps), cam switch;
  - ARM-rail sensing, watchdog, a 0.96" OLED (state, range, shots left, last speed), a buzzer (chirp), eyes (2× GC9A01, or WS2812 to save pins and money).
- **Aiming:** by eye over a printed sight on the head; the person sits on a tape mark (every 0.25 m) and the operator dials that range.
- **USB:** report only (STAT, SHOT, FAULT, JSON shot lines) for an optional laptop logger.

**Phase 2 (face tracking)** adds:

- **Compute board:** Raspberry Pi 5 1 GB running Trixie Lite and Python 3.13, with an OV5647 CSI camera (D-007; out of stock in India, bought only for phase 2). Camera, MediaPipe FaceLandmarker, range estimate, aim controller, vision fire control, modes, UI and I2S sound.
- **Link:** USB serial carrying a line protocol with sequence numbers and CRC8, plus a 20 Hz heartbeat ([protocol](docs/architecture.md#microcontroller-protocol-draft)).
- **Camera** on the turret head, tilted about 10° down from the barrel (D-003). Visual servoing holds the mouth at a range-dependent offset below the barrel axis, and the camera-on-head arrangement cancels gear play.

**Both phases:**

- **Launcher** (D-001): a snail-cam spring push sled. The pellet rides the sled from the start (push, never hit), and a bumper stops the sled. A self-locking JGY370 worm cocks it, and reversing the cam decocks it.
- **Feed** (D-006): an orientation-locking stack above a spring-return shuttle, with a chamber IR sensor. A jam triggers reverse, retry, then fault.
- **Power:** 12 V 5 A in → fuse → 5 V buck for logic; the E-stop feeds the turret rail, and the ARM switch feeds the launcher rail.

## Conventions

- **Python:** ≥ 3.11, type hints, dataclasses. SI units inside (m, s, rad, kg). Put units in names (`range_m`, `pan_deg`, `t_ms`); degrees appear only at config and UI boundaries.
- **Hardware abstraction:** all hardware sits behind interfaces with fakes. Phase 1: the firmware's fire-control, joystick and holdover logic is plain Python that runs under pytest on the PC before it goes on the board (keep it MicroPython-compatible: no dataclasses or typing at runtime there). Phase 2: a feature runs on the Windows PC with a webcam and `--sim` before it touches hardware.
- **Traceability:** cite requirement IDs in code and tests (`# REQ-SAF-017`). Decisions change only by adding a new D-entry.
- **Config:** tunables in `config/desuu.yaml`, per-robot calibration (sight zero, holdover table) in `config/calibration.yaml`. Never hard-code calibration.
- **Shot log:** one JSON line per shot (time, range, angles, gate results, measured speed); the microcontroller prints them over USB, and the laptop logger writes them under `logs/`.
- **Research:** keep sources and mark claims (unverified) or (estimate). Prices go stale fast: re-check before ordering.
- **Ballistics:** after changing an assumption, run `python tools/ballistics_sim.py --out docs/research/ballistics.md`. It takes about 10 s, and every assumption is a flag. The §7 table seeds the phase 1 holdover table.
- **Git:** commit only when the owner asks. Remote: `origin` on GitHub (public), branch `main`.

## Implementation gotchas

**Phase 1 (microcontroller)**

- Pico ADC: only GP26–28 are analog, and the KY-023 needs two of them. Give the joystick 3.3 V, not 5 V, or the ADC clips.
- KY-023 centres drift and vary per stick: calibrate centre and span at boot with the stick released, and treat readings outside the plausible band as "controller disconnected".
- Chronograph edges need PIO (Pico) or the capture peripheral (ESP32): MicroPython interrupt jitter (~50 µs) would add 0.5 % to the speed reading.
- Pin budget is tight on a Pico once the GC9A01 eyes are added; WS2812 eyes free four pins.

**Phase 2 (vision).** Sources: [vision-compute.md](docs/research/vision-compute.md), [compute-camera-india.md](docs/research/compute-camera-india.md).

*MediaPipe*

- Use the Tasks `FaceLandmarker` only: `mp.solutions` (face_mesh) was removed in 0.10.30.
- Use `mediapipe` ≥ 1.1 on Python 3.13. Release 1.2.x renamed some Tasks fields and kept deprecated aliases.
- Ignore old tutorials that pin 0.10.18 or run Bookworm.
- Run in VIDEO mode, with your own capture thread. LIVE_STREAM has reports of hanging.
- On Linux, inference runs on one thread. Extra worker processes raise FPS but don't cut per-frame latency.
- The built-in BlazeFace detector letterboxes the frame to 128 px, so a face at 2 m is about 7–9 px. Feed it a centre or tracked crop (or use full-range BlazeFace or YuNet to find the face first).
- For aiming precision, give the landmarker a full-resolution face crop, not a frame downscaled to 640 px.

*Face landmarks and distance*

- Landmark indices: inner lips 13 (upper) and 14 (lower); inner mouth corners 78 and 308; iris centres 468 and 473.
- Starting mouth rule: open when `jawOpen ≥ 0.45` and MAR ≥ 0.35; closed when `jawOpen < 0.30` or MAR < 0.25. Calibrate per person.
- The facial-matrix depth assumes a 63° vertical FOV. Rescale it by `tan(31.5°) / tan(VFOV_real/2)`.
- Range needs per-person calibration. Uncalibrated cues are about 4–6 % off, against a 2.5 % budget.

*Raspberry Pi 5 setup*

- Create the venv with `--system-site-packages`, because picamera2, libcamera and lgpio come only from apt.
- pip's OpenCV 5.1 needs NumPy ≥ 2. Trixie is fine; Bookworm breaks.
- Picamera2 uses DRM format names: `RGB888` is B,G,R byte order, so request `BGR888` for RGB and verify it.
- pigpio doesn't work on the Pi 5, and lgpio PWM is software-timed. All actuators go through the microcontroller anyway.
