# Launcher & Prior Art Research

Last researched: 2026-11-25 (web research; verify before buying or building)

Conventions: **(calc)** = my arithmetic or simulation from cited inputs (level shot, 1.5 g pellet, C_D 0.72 unless stated; the fuller model with the mouth above the muzzle is [ballistics.md](ballistics.md)). **(unverified)** = no source found. **(snippet)** = seen only in a search-result excerpt because the page blocks automated reading. Source years are given; treat anything before 2024 as possibly outdated.

## Key takeaways for the design

- **Pick one hard-coated, non-liquid 1.4–1.5 g pellet and design around it.** Extra (AU/UK) and PUR are 1.4 g and Lotte Xylitol is 1.5 g. Mentos Pure Fresh is 2.0 g with a *liquid centre*, and Ice Breakers Ice Cubes are 2.3 g. In the US, Orbit, Extra and Trident are mostly *sticks*, not pellets. A 2.3 g piece at 6 m/s carries 41 mJ, which is over the 40 mJ cap used in ballistics.md. No brand publishes piece dimensions; the only published envelope is the US federal spec (14–25 × 8.9–16 × 4–9 mm for 1.1–1.6 g). Caliper 20–30 pieces before designing the magazine.
- **Recommendation: a snail-cam-cocked spring "push sled", where the pellet touches the pusher from the start (no hammer blow).** The spring physically caps the energy. Parked just after the drop it holds only its small preload, so disarmed means nearly de-energised. Parts cost under ~$30 (unverified). If the moving mass is about 3× the pellet, a ±10 % pellet-mass error changes exit speed by only about ±1.25 % (calc). A dual closed-loop flywheel is the runner-up. A single flywheel, pneumatics and solenoid kickers fit poorly (§3).
- **Exit-speed SD needs to be about 1 %. The best launchers in other fields reach that, but only when run at their design energy.** Regulated-air paintball: 2.0 % SD. Strong-spring foam blaster: 1.0 %. Brushless flywheel blaster: 1.5–3.4 %. Two-roller tennis machine: CV 1–2 %. The same launchers run weak or unregulated do much worse: the stock weak spring gives 13 %, unregulated CO2 12.7 %, an old stock flywheel blaster ~11 %. Our 4–7 m/s is 4–20× slower than those darts and paintballs, so friction and contact variation weigh more. Use low-friction guides and generous spring energy, and put it on a chronograph early.
- **Error per unit (calc):** each 1 % of speed error moves the impact about 4 mm at 1 m (5 m/s) and about 9 mm at 2 m (7 m/s). Each 0.5° of pointing error is 8.7 mm per metre. The 3 cm circle at 1 m (±15 mm) allows only ±0.86° in total. At 2 m it is probably out of reach with camera-only ranging.
- **Range estimation is the largest single error.** MediaPipe Iris reports a 4.3 % mean depth error (2020). That alone moves the impact ±9 mm at 1 m (5 m/s) and ±20 mm at 2 m (7 m/s) (calc), which sits between ballistics.md's target (2.5 %) and pessimistic (6 %) budgets. Consider a ToF sensor or per-user calibration. An *over*-estimated range lands the pellet high, toward the eyes, so bias range estimates short.
- **Faster and flatter is more accurate but carries more energy.** Going from 5 to 7 m/s halves the speed and range sensitivity at 1 m but doubles kinetic energy (19 → 37 mJ for 1.5 g). A fixed exit speed with elevation-only aiming is the simplest and most repeatable setup.
- **Avoid spin.** A single wheel pressing the pellet against a rail spins it at spin parameter S ≈ 1 (calc). Sphere data give lift coefficient C_L ≈ S, and even C_L = 0.25 shifts the impact about 6 mm at 1 m and 24 mm at 2 m (calc). Speed-matched dual wheels give S ≈ (v1−v2)/(v1+v2) ≈ 0.
- **Drag matters at 2 m; tumbling scatter much less.** Reynolds number is about 2,600–7,400 and C_D is roughly 0.7 (calc, Bagheri & Bonadonna correlation). Leaving drag out of the aim misses by about 5 mm at 1 m and 20 mm at 2 m, while a ±30 % C_D spread from tumbling adds only ±1.5 mm and ±6 mm (calc).
- **Prior projects failed on the ammunition and the feeder, not the vision.** Popcorn was dropped for its irregular shapes. Marshmallow aim tables stopped working after an hour out of the bag. Petcube needs uniform, dry, hard treats, and its landing distance still varies by up to 30 cm per shot. Treat flingers jam where the loader meets the chute. HTX Studio's "Gummy" (2025) already fires gum when a mouth opens, but no mechanism is published.
- **Safety patterns:** fit hardware E-stops that bypass software (Munchkin, 2025), and fire only after tracking error stays small for a dwell time. Commenters on earlier candy launchers raised eye and choking risks. If the toy-projectile threshold of 0.08 J (EN 71-1 / ASTM F963, unverified) applies, speed must stay ≤10 m/s for 1.5 g and ≤8.3 m/s for 2.3 g.

- **Read the consistency data as:** every mechanism reaches 1–2 % when built well and run at its design energy. Running far below it (stock weak spring, lowest flywheel speed level, unregulated gas) multiplies scatter by 3–10×.
- **Hardware speed cap:** a spring caps energy physically. A flywheel needs a motor whose no-load rpm at maximum supply voltage cannot exceed the cap; with a 6000 rpm motor and a 2-in wheel, surface speed is about 16 m/s (calc). Pneumatics need a relief valve.
- **Timing:** solenoid pulses and valve dwell should come from a microcontroller or RP2040 PIO, not Linux user space on the Pi 5. The Bots saw pulse variability even at 1 µs resolution.
- **Flywheel energy (calc):** one 40 mm, 10 g printed wheel at 6 m/s surface speed stores about 0.09 J, so a pair stores about 0.18 J. A 37 mJ shot plus slip losses takes about 20 % of that, a wheel-speed droop of about 10 %. Use heavier or larger wheels and let them recover fully between shots (AIMY: ramp up for ≥0.5 s).

## 4. Aerodynamics

- **Regime (calc):** Re = ρvd/μ ≈ 2,600 (4 m/s, 10 mm) to 7,400 (8 m/s, 14 mm). That is close to the Newton regime, where C_D is roughly constant.
- **Drag coefficients:** a sphere averages about 0.46 (0.38–0.50) in the Newton regime. For non-spherical particles in gases, orientation is random and secondary motions (tumbling) are fully developed. Shape changes drag by 48–77 %; roughness by about 10 % ([Bagheri & Bonadonna 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.08787)). A flat plate facing the flow is 1.17–1.28 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drag_coefficient)).
- **Pellet estimate (calc):** the Bagheri–Bonadonna Newton-regime fit is log₁₀k_N = 0.45·(−log₁₀F_N)^0.99, with F_N = S·d_eq³/(L²·I²) and C_D ≈ 0.463·k_N. It has 10.9 % mean error and is valid for density ratios of 150–2130. For a 17 × 11 × 7 mm, ~1 cm³ pellet it gives d_eq = 12.4 mm and **C_D ≈ 0.72 on the equivalent-sphere area**. That makes C_D·A ≈ 87 mm², close to ballistics.md's 0.90 × 110 = 99 mm².
- **Drag size (calc, 1.5 g):** deceleration is 0.56 m/s² at 4 m/s, 0.87 at 5, 1.25 at 6, 1.70 at 7 and 2.23 at 8 (0.06–0.23 g). Speed loss is about 3.4 % over 1 m at 5 m/s and 6.6 % over 2 m at 7 m/s.
- **Tumbling:** tumbling plates generate lift from their own rotation, with circulation dominated by a term proportional to angular velocity ([Andersen, Pesavento & Wang 2005](https://dragonfly.tam.cornell.edu/publications/2005_JFM_Andersen_Pesavento_Wang_a.pdf), snippet). A tumbling pillow pellet can therefore drift sideways in a random direction, which shows up as scatter rather than bias (magnitude unverified).
- **Magnus:** at low speed C_L ≈ S over S = 0.4–1.0, roughly independent of Re. Smooth balls can show a *reverse* Magnus effect, with C_L < 0 for S = 0.15–0.55 at 55 mph ([Nathan 2008](http://baseball.physics.illinois.edu/AJPFeb08.pdf)), so the sign for a smooth coated pellet is uncertain (unverified). A single wheel against a rail rolls the pellet, giving ω ≈ v/r and S ≈ 1. For a 7 mm-thick pellet at 5 m/s that is about 1,400 rad/s, or 13,600 rpm (calc). Dual wheels with a 5 % speed mismatch give S ≈ 0.025, which is negligible (calc).
- **Thrown-candy experiments:** none found (unverified). The closest evidence is anecdotal: popcorn shape (Popinator) and marshmallow condition (Olin) dominated their scatter.

## 5. Snail-cam spring launchers

- **Cam profile:** use a parabolic spiral, r = k·√θ + r_min, where k = 0.0527 per unit of lift over 360° with θ in degrees. It keeps motor torque roughly constant, with 50 % of the lift in the first 25 % of rotation. An Archimedean spiral loads the motor more and more and slows the end of the stroke. Spring preload changes the ideal profile ([runamok snail cam](https://runamok.tech/AskAaron/snail_cam.html)).
- **Follower and drop:** sliding friction "can be considerable". A UHMW cam helps, and a needle-bearing roller follower "can completely eliminate the problem" (runamok). After the vertical drop, give the follower a flat landing region; a drop followed immediately by a steep rise is nearly impossible to follow. Put a stable flat or detent at the ready point so holding it needs no motor torque (runamok). Make the drop height at least the full stroke plus clearance, so the released sled never touches the cam (engineering, unverified).
- **Motor sizing:** torque = average spring force × lift ÷ 2π. Pick a gearmotor with about 3× that as stall torque, and stop it with a microswitch at the loaded position (runamok). Worked example (calc): a 37 mJ shot with moving mass 3× the pellet needs about 150 mJ of spring energy. Over a 25 mm stroke that is 6 N average force and 0.024 N·m (0.24 kgf·cm), so aim for ≥0.7 kgf·cm stall torque. Typical N20 worm gearmotor listings claim about 1–5 kgf·cm, and JGY-370 listings claim much more at 6–12 V (unverified).
- **Self-locking worm motors:** whether a worm is self-locking depends on lead angle, pressure angle and friction, and multi-start worms may back-drive ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worm_drive)). Don't rely on the worm as the only safety lock under vibration (unverified). Hold the cocked state with the cam detent plus a mechanical sear or pin tied to the arm/disarm switch. Better still, stay uncocked while disarmed and cock only after arming; this costs up to one cam turn of latency.
- **Energy transfer (calc):** in a push design the pellet leaves at the sled's speed, so η ≈ m / (m + M_sled + m_spring/3). The spring's effective mass is one third of its mass ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effective_mass_%28spring%E2%80%93mass_system%29)). ER-Force's kicker model likewise uses the summed plunger + ball mass.
- **Sensitivity to pellet mass (calc):** dv/v = −½·dm/(m + M_eff). For M_eff/m = 0, 1, 3 and 5, a +10 % heavier pellet loses 5 %, 2.5 %, 1.25 % and 0.83 % of speed, at efficiencies of 100, 50, 25 and 17 %. A deliberately heavy sled (≈3× pellet, ~4–5 g) is the cheapest consistency fix.
- **Low-energy friction:** the same springer went from 1.0 % SD to 13 % SD on a weak spring (§3). Use a stiffer spring with margin, a sled on bearings, bushings or polished rods, and no O-ring air piston (engineering).
- **End-of-stroke damping:** with M_eff = 3m, about 75 % of the spring energy (~110 mJ) has to be absorbed every shot (calc). Use a urethane or sorbothane bumper so the sled can't rebound into the pellet. An option is to fix the sled to the spring so the pellet separates at the spring's free length (peak sled speed) with no hard impact, and damp the sled's oscillation afterward (engineering, unverified). Push rather than strike: impact adds restitution variance and can crack the coating (engineering).
- **Velocity adjustment:** a preload screw (manual or motorised) works, but a fixed speed with elevation-only aiming is preferable. On a level shot, 0.5–2 m needs about 3–12° of elevation at 7 m/s and 4–17° at 6 m/s (calc). For a mouth above the muzzle, see ballistics.md.
